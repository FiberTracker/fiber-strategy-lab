Verizon challenge: Omni Fiber and legacy Lit Fiber

As of: September 20, 2026

Citizens status as of September 20, 2026. Omni announced the transaction on June 1, 2026. A July 11 Pennsylvania transfer-of-control notice shows applications for approval; no final approval or consummation notice was located. Final legal closing remains unresolved in the reviewed public record.

Decision

Low conviction for a whole-platform acquisition. Medium conviction for selective commercial diligence. Omni/Lit offers real regional FTTH construction capability, but the burden of proof is now higher. Verizon already closed Frontier, owns an MDU-specific capability through Starry, and has a third-party-funded exclusive retail structure through Eaton Fiber. Omni/Lit is differentiated only if it can prove a large address-level non-overlap perimeter, attractive seasoned-cohort returns, or transferable building and transport rights that Verizon cannot obtain more cheaply through those existing channels.

That conclusion is an analytical inference, not evidence of buyer interest.

What is verified

[P] Frontier closed. Verizon completed the acquisition on January 20, 2026. Its filings report approximately $9.4 billion of net cash consideration and $12.9 billion of assumed debt, or approximately $22.3 billion of aggregate consideration. Verizon said the combined network exceeded 30 million fiber passings. Frontier results have been consolidated since closing. S1S2S3

[P] Starry closed. Verizon completed the acquisition on January 30, 2026. Its 10-K calls the consideration insignificant and identifies Starry as a fixed wireless provider serving MDUs in five U.S. markets. At the October 8, 2025 announcement, Starry reported nearly 100,000 MDU customers in Boston, New York, Los Angeles, Denver and Washington, D.C. S2S4

[P] Eaton is a capital-light FTTH retail model, not a conventional acquisition. The October 27, 2025 agreement assigned network funding, construction, maintenance and installation to Eaton. Verizon controls sales, marketing and end-user service and receives residential retail exclusivity during the build plus an undisclosed additional period. The July 29, 2026 joint release describes Verizon as exclusive retailer for residential and small-business fiber while Eaton funds, builds, maintains and operates the network. S5S6

The same July release announced a $1.5 billion investment to fund Eaton's proposed acquisition of Ripple Fiber and its next expansion phase. Ripple was under a definitive agreement with closing targeted before year-end 2026. It was announced, not verified closed, as of this review. More than one million locations is a forward target, not actual passings. Wholesale pricing, volume commitments, market schedule and the precise post-build exclusivity term are unknown. S6

Strategy, capital and the acquisition hurdle

[P] Strategy: Verizon has stated a 40 million to 50 million fiber-passing objective over time, at least 2 million organic passings in 2026, and willingness to use builds, acquisitions and partnerships. Its September 8, 2026 Corning agreement covers more than 80 million miles of fiber through 2032 for consumer broadband and a shared backbone serving cell towers, businesses and data centers. S7S8

[P] Capital facts: At June 30, 2026, Verizon reported $165.2 billion of total debt, 2.5x net unsecured debt to adjusted EBITDA, $8.2 billion of first-half capex, $9.5 billion of acquisition cash outlays and $381 million of Frontier acquisition and integration charges. It repaid approximately $12.4 billion of Frontier debt principal during the half. Full-year capex guidance remained $16.0 billion to $16.5 billion. S1

[Inference] Verizon is not demonstrably financing-constrained. Management has said it has balance-sheet flexibility. The constraint is relative return, management attention and integration capacity. A whole Omni/Lit purchase must beat three funded alternatives: higher penetration and expansion inside Frontier, Verizon's organic builds, and Eaton's third-party-funded wholesale model.

Omni/Lit perimeter and overlap

[P] Scale with units and dates: Omni said on December 1, 2025 that it had invested more than $400 million, served customers in approximately 60 communities, had construction in approximately 25 communities and was on track to reach approximately 340,000 locations by year-end 2025. The 340,000 figure was a target, not a reported actual. On June 1, 2026, Omni said cumulative network investment exceeded $500 million and announced the Citizens Fiber acquisition plus plans for nearly 200,000 additional locations across six western Pennsylvania counties. Subscribers, actual passings, penetration and EBITDA remain undisclosed. S10S11

Omni and Lit announced their merger on November 20, 2024; the announcement did not state an exact legal close date. Later public materials establish integration and identify Omni as the surviving entity by fiscal 2025. Lit's disclosed perimeter was Medina County, Ohio and a Brownsville, Texas build. Both companies were then majority-owned by Oak Hill, with Stephens Capital Partners and The Pritzker Organization remaining Lit shareholders. Current fully diluted ownership is unknown. S9

[P] City-level overlap: Current company pages show Omni and Frontier both listing Akron, Ashland, Dover, Huron, London, Medina, New Philadelphia, Norwalk, Sandusky, Seville and Toledo in Ohio. In Michigan, both list Lambertville, Milan and Temperance, although Omni labels those Michigan markets as under construction or coming soon. S12S13S14

[D] A city match is not same-address fiber overlap. The current selected-filer intersection table reports exact D25 location-level intersections between Omni legal filer 480067 and selected Verizon/Frontier legal filers in Michigan, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Texas. It measures 33,130 shared Ohio locations, or 21.1% of Omni's D25 Ohio availability, and zero shared locations in the three other measured states. These are December 31, 2025 technology-50 residential-capable R/X availability sets for selected legal filers only. They exclude cable, fixed wireless, other independent fiber, subscribers, current construction and future overbuild, and do not establish current sellable whitespace or a valuation deduction. Subscriber files and cluster capex schedules remain unavailable; western Pennsylvania and Brownsville differentiation is still unproven.

[Inference] City-level co-presence is a diligence trigger. It does not establish same-address overlap, duplicated network, or a valuation premium deduction. Any perimeter adjustment must use address-level serviceability, connected customers and remaining capital requirements, not city-name matches.

Why MDU ability matters, and why Starry weakens it

[P] Omni publishes two blank right-of-entry forms with different duration terms. The July-path form contemplates perpetual access and express successor assignment. The September-path form contemplates a 20-year initial term followed by one-year renewals subject to notice. Neither blank form proves executed property rights or a universal current standard. Executed property agreements control, and the later URL does not prove that earlier executed agreements were replaced.

[Inference] MDU competence can matter because one property agreement can unlock many serviceable units per lateral and create dense mobile-plus-home cross-sell. It can also overcome a real bottleneck: landlord access and inside wiring. However, Starry weakens a generic claim that Verizon needs Omni to solve MDUs. Verizon bought Starry specifically for MDU and urban deployments and described its millimeter-wave architecture as quicker and less expensive to deploy. Omni must therefore prove transferable access in unique secondary markets, strong building-level take rates, or FTTH economics and service quality that beat Starry's fixed wireless alternative. Starry is a substitute for the acquisition rationale, not proof that all MDU FTTH is redundant.

Do not conflate FTTH wholesale with mobile backhaul

The Eaton arrangement is wholesale FTTH access with exclusive Verizon retail rights. It does not establish fiber-to-tower or mobile-backhaul rights. Omni markets dedicated business internet, and its Pennsylvania tariff permits point-to-point Ethernet and possible dark fiber on an individual-case basis. No reviewed public source disclosed tower contracts, tower laterals, route miles, protected rings, spare strands or carrier-grade backhaul SLAs. S5S6S16S17

Residential passings cannot be counted as backhaul assets. A mobile-backhaul case requires route-level proof of on-net or near-net towers, diversity, capacity, restoration terms, contract tenure and incremental lateral capex.

Three possible structures and two-sided economics

  1. Selective asset acquisition with overlap carveouts and earnout. Verizon buys only verified non-overlap clusters, customers and required backbone. It gains immediate retail and convergence economics but funds purchase price, systems migration and completion capex. Oak Hill receives liquidity and can retain overlapping or early-build markets, while deferred consideration depends on verified serviceable locations, connected customers, churn and EBITDA. This prevents announced targets from being paid as completed network.

  2. Eaton-style exclusive FTTH wholesale conversion. Omni/Oak Hill retains and funds infrastructure; Verizon becomes exclusive retailer in approved non-overlap zones. Verizon preserves capital and gains Fios-branded customer economics, but pays wholesale charges and may owe volume or marketing support. Omni gains an anchor and potentially cheaper financing, but surrenders retail brand, customer ownership and some upside while retaining build and SLA risk. It must beat Eaton on geography or economics.

  3. Mobile backhaul, dark-fiber or IRU contract only. Verizon purchases route-specific capacity where Omni paths are cheaper or faster than self-build, without acquiring FTTH retail operations. Verizon pays recurring lease or upfront IRU consideration and demands carrier-grade diversity and restoration. Omni monetizes spare strands and improves utilization but funds tower laterals and operational upgrades. No value should be assigned until routes, towers, capacity, contracts and capex are verified.

Five diligence questions that can falsify the thesis

  1. After BSL deduplication, how many Omni/Lit locations and subscribers are outside Verizon, Frontier and committed Eaton markets, and how many are merely planned or under construction?
  2. By build cohort, what are actual cost per passing, cost per connection, penetration, ARPU, churn, contribution margin, bad debt and remaining capex, reconciled to audited financials and debt reporting?
  3. How many MDU buildings and units have executed transferable ROEs, and where do building install cost, take rate and churn beat Starry's fixed wireless economics?
  4. Which Verizon-relevant towers, small cells, hubs or enterprise sites are on-net or near-net, and what route diversity, contracts, spare strands, SLAs and lateral capex support a backhaul valuation separate from FTTH?
  5. What change-of-control, lien, municipal, grant, customer-transfer and exclusivity restrictions arise from Omni's debt, Brownsville arrangements, Citizens acquisition and Lit integration, and can clusters be carved out without stranded cost?
